# Australian Government Context Gate

Load this reference when the work is for a **Commonwealth** entity or when whole-of-government digital investment, digital service, procurement, risk, protective-security, cyber-security, or architecture obligations may affect the project decision.

This reference provides **routing and decision logic**, not a frozen compliance checklist. The cited external sources were verified on 14 September 2026. Re-check the live authoritative source before making a compliance, threshold, mandate, exemption, or version-specific claim.

## 1. First classify the jurisdiction and entity

Do not use “Australian Government” as a synonym for every Australian public-sector body.

1. Identify whether the project is Commonwealth, state/territory, local government, or cross-jurisdictional.
2. For Commonwealth work, identify the entity type where it affects applicability (for example, non-corporate vs corporate Commonwealth entity).
3. Identify agency-specific Accountable Authority Instructions, delegations, security policy, architecture standards, procurement procedures, delivery/assurance method, and investment governance.
4. If these are unavailable and they could change the recommendation, mark the relevant point `Needs Human Review`.

Never apply a Commonwealth mandatory policy to a state, territory, or local entity merely because the project is government IT.

## 2. Digital and ICT investment oversight

The Digital Transformation Agency describes the Whole-of-Government Digital and ICT Investment Oversight Framework (IOF) as covering digital/ICT-enabled investments from early planning through delivery and benefits realisation.

When a Commonwealth digital/ICT investment may be in IOF scope:

- verify current IOF applicability and agency obligations;
- map the project’s decision to the relevant investment state or assurance point;
- connect policy intent, delivery readiness, architecture, sourcing, benefits, and investment evidence;
- engage the relevant agency/DTA process early rather than creating a parallel project-only governance path;
- do not claim DTA approval, contestability outcome, assurance rating, or investment status without evidence.

**Authoritative starting points**

- DTA IOF: <https://www.dta.gov.au/our-initiatives/digital-and-ict-investment-oversight-framework-iof>
- DTA corporate plan/current IOF context: <https://www.dta.gov.au/corporate-plan-2026-27>

## 3. Digital service and experience obligations

DTA’s Digital Experience Policy and associated standards govern defined digital-service contexts and have phased applicability. The Digital Service Standard 2.0 is outcomes-based.

For a digital service:

- verify whether the policy/standard applies to the service type and lifecycle stage;
- translate applicable criteria into delivery evidence, acceptance/assurance activities, backlog work, service measures, and ownership;
- ensure user research, inclusion/accessibility, trust, reuse/common capability, harm prevention, monitoring, and ongoing relevance are considered where applicable;
- do not assert “DSS compliant” from a checklist alone: identify evidence and the applicable compliance/assurance process.

**Authoritative starting points**

- DTA Digital Experience Policy update: <https://www.dta.gov.au/updates/updates-digital-experience-policy-and-suite-standards>
- DTA Digital Service Standard rollout: <https://www.dta.gov.au/articles/digital-service-standard-now-fully-effect>
- DTA DSS resources: <https://www.dta.gov.au/node/1637>

## 4. Architecture and reuse

The Australian Government Architecture (AGA) is a collection of digital artefacts and guidance intended to help align digital investment with government direction and is connected to the IOF.

When architecture/reuse is material:

- check applicable AGA policies, standards, patterns, designs, and common capabilities;
- test whether reuse is feasible before proposing bespoke capability;
- record exceptions and trade-offs rather than assuming “reuse” or “build” by default;
- keep architecture decisions traceable to outcomes, interoperability, security, lifecycle cost, and operational ownership.

**Authoritative starting point**

- DTA AGA: <https://www.dta.gov.au/our-initiatives/australian-government-architecture>

## 5. Procurement and sourcing

The Department of Finance states that the Commonwealth Procurement Rules (CPRs) govern procurement by relevant entities and that applicability depends on entity and procurement context. Entity Accountable Authority Instructions and operational guidance also matter.

Before advising procurement:

1. Identify the entity and the current CPR version.
2. Confirm estimated value, procurement category, relevant threshold, panel/whole-of-government arrangement, exemptions, connected policies, and entity-specific instructions where material.
3. Distinguish mandatory rules (`must`) from guidance/good practice.
4. Check probity, value-for-money, competition, risk, accountability/transparency, due diligence, contract-management, security/data, and transition considerations that actually apply.
5. Do not invent a limited-tender justification, exemption, threshold, evaluation criterion, or approval.
6. Treat schedule pressure as a planning constraint, not authority to bypass procurement controls.

**Authoritative starting points**

- Department of Finance CPRs: <https://www.finance.gov.au/government/procurement/commonwealth-procurement-rules>
- Procurement framework: <https://www.finance.gov.au/government/procurement/commonwealth-procurement-rules/procurement-framework>
- Procurement process considerations: <https://www.finance.gov.au/government/procurement/buying-australian-government/procurement-process-considerations>

## 6. Commonwealth risk management

The Department of Finance’s Commonwealth Risk Management Policy supports the PGPA Act risk obligation. Its applicability differs between non-corporate and corporate Commonwealth entities.

For Commonwealth project risk:

- verify the entity’s mandatory policy position and internal risk framework;
- connect project risks to entity risk appetite/tolerance, control effectiveness, shared risks, emerging risks, and escalation;
- identify when a project-level treatment depends on another entity, supplier, program, policy owner, or enterprise control;
- avoid scoring or accepting risk outside the relevant delegation.

**Authoritative starting points**

- Commonwealth Risk Management Policy: <https://www.finance.gov.au/government/comcover/risk-services/management/commonwealth-risk-management-policy>
- Commonwealth Risk Management Framework: <https://www.finance.gov.au/government/comcover/commonwealth-risk-management-framework>

## 7. Protective security and cyber security

PSPF Release 2026 is current as at this reference’s verification date. The PSPF applies differently by entity type and context; it is mandatory for specified Commonwealth entities and may apply contractually or by agreement in other contexts. ASD’s Information Security Manual (ISM) is a cyber-security framework that organisations apply through risk management.

For security-sensitive project decisions:

- identify PSPF applicability, security classification/protective-marking implications, applicable PSPF standards/technical manuals/directions, and agency security policy;
- identify the current ISM release and applicable controls for the system risk context;
- include security assurance, system authorisation/accreditation processes where locally required, evidence owners, residual risk, supplier obligations, and operational handover;
- do not treat Essential Eight, ISM, PSPF, or a generic security checklist as interchangeable;
- do not approve exceptions on behalf of the accountable security/risk authority.

**Authoritative starting points**

- PSPF: <https://www.protectivesecurity.gov.au/>
- PSPF applicability: <https://www.protectivesecurity.gov.au/about/applying-protective-security-policy-framework>
- PSPF annual releases: <https://www.protectivesecurity.gov.au/pspf-annual-release>
- ASD ISM: <https://www.cyber.gov.au/ism>

## 8. Decision output for Commonwealth context

When one or more branches above apply, make the project-management answer explicit about:

- **Applicable context:** jurisdiction, entity, policy/standard/framework branch.
- **Verified requirement vs guidance:** what is mandatory, advisory, local, contractual, or still unknown.
- **Project impact:** lifecycle, scope, schedule, cost, procurement, architecture, security, quality, service outcomes, resources, benefits, and dependencies.
- **Decision owner:** delegation or accountable role, if evidenced.
- **Required evidence:** what must exist before approval or assurance.
- **Next checkpoint:** the next investment, procurement, assurance, governance, or operational decision.
- **NHR:** any applicability, currency, exemption, threshold, or approval point not verified.

## Common failure modes

- Treating all Australian public-sector bodies as subject to Commonwealth policy.
- Copying framework criteria into a plan without identifying evidence or ownership.
- Assuming a project board can override procurement, security, legal, or accountable-authority delegations.
- Quoting a remembered threshold or policy version without checking the current source.
- Treating “agile” as a reason to remove assurance or investment controls.
- Treating a successful go-live as the end of benefits, service monitoring, or residual-risk ownership.
